# Attention is global. Money is local.

I spent a few weeks building a consumption database across six markets to settle an argument I kept
having with myself.

The argument was about a sentence I've heard in almost every Indian media conversation: that we have
the world's largest audience, so the opportunity is obviously enormous. I wanted to know whether
that's actually true once you follow the money. So I collected time-spent data and revenue data for
India, the US, the UK, Japan, South Korea and Brazil across five formats: linear TV, streaming video,
social and short video, gaming, and music and audio.

Then I divided one by the other, because comparing a $30 billion market to a $2 trillion market
directly tells you nothing. What I wanted was revenue per hour of attention.

**India earns roughly $3.60 for every 1,000 hours its people spend consuming media. The United
States earns roughly $307.**

That's about 86x. The GDP per capita gap between the two countries is about 33x on the numbers I
used. So income explains part of what I found. It doesn't explain all of it, and the part it doesn't
explain is the interesting part.

The consumption side isn't in question. Indians spent 1.23 trillion hours on smartphones in 2025 and
about 59% of that was entertainment (FICCI-EY). India has 601 million OTT users and 216.5 million
paid OTT subscriptions, which is the third largest paying base in the world (PwC). On any measure of
attention, India is a top-three market.

The revenue side is a different story. Each of those 216.5 million subscriptions is worth **$7.30 a
year** to the platform. India's whole OTT industry is a $2.2 billion market. US video streaming is
$113 billion, on a subscriber base only about 13% larger. Indian digital advertising works out to
roughly $8 per person; in the US it's around $1,000 (Redseer).

The paying base already exists. The price doesn't.

Three things seem to follow, and none of them is "find more users."

**Growth has to come from yield.** Adding an Indian streaming subscriber is now cheap relative to
what they generate. One extra dollar of ARPU across 216.5 million subscriptions is $216 million of
revenue, which is more than the entire Indian theatrical box office in 2025. If your plan's main
lever is user acquisition, you're optimising an input that stopped being scarce.

**The same screen is worth a lot more if you sell it differently.** Indian linear TV advertising
fell 10% in 2025 while connected TV advertising grew 42%, to about ₹99 billion. In the UK, total
in-home video time fell by 7 minutes last year, but linear viewing fell 14 minutes while
broadcaster on-demand went up 3. In the US, streaming passed broadcast and cable combined for the
first time, at 47.3% of TV time, while total TV time stayed flat at around 3.7 hours a day. Same
conclusion in all three markets: advertisers aren't leaving the screen, they're leaving the schedule.

**Gaming and live are the only formats where time and money both grow.** Global games are a $188.8
billion market, bigger than film and recorded music combined, but the per-player economics are wildly
uneven. Japan gets $17.6 billion from 74 million players. India gets about $1.5 billion from 555
million (Newzoo, Lumikai). Live is similar: organised live events in India grew 44% in a single year.
Neither carries the content amortisation drag of scripted video, and both collect money at the point
of consumption rather than months later.

The finding that surprised me most is that there's no such thing as a global consumer to benchmark
against. Japan's population spends 217 minutes a day on linear TV and about 50 minutes on social
media, the lowest of any major market. Brazil spends 229 minutes a day on social, the highest in the
world. In South Korea, gaming is bigger than television. India sits at 140 minutes of social and 65
of TV, which makes it a mobile-first market that doesn't look like a TV market or a social-first
market either.

So the usual benchmarking exercise, where you take the global average and find your gap, doesn't
work here. The average of six markets that disagree this much doesn't describe any of them. It's more
useful to compare a market to its own past, and to the two or three markets that actually share its
structural conditions.

**One caveat, and it's a real one.** The time-spent data is the weakest link in all of this. Ofcom
measures in-home video on TV sets. Nielsen measures the TV screen. DataReportal asks people to
self-report their internet time. Each is good for its own purpose and none was designed to be
compared with the others. The revenue figures are firmer, coming from industry bodies, filings and
audit-grade reports. So treat the size of the 86x gap as an estimate and the direction as the
finding.

The direction I'd defend. India has solved distribution, production and audience. It hasn't solved
price. For the next decade of Indian media, the question worth asking stops being "how many people
watched this" and becomes "what was that hour actually worth."
